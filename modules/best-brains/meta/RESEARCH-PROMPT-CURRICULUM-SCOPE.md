## 0. Why this exists (the decision it feeds)

Our reconstruction of this programme ships **5 levels (A–E) × 24 weeks = 120 weeks of content**, where one week = a 5-day arc (Day 1 concept, Days 2–3 fluency + discrimination, Day 4 application, Day 5 non-computational + weekly check).

The age bands we assigned are **A** 3–5 · **B** 6–7 · **C** 7–9 · **D** ~9–10 · **E** 10–12. That is roughly **ten years of childhood covered by 120 weeks — about 12 weeks of material per year.** A weekly programme needs roughly 40.

The structure fails from both directions:
- If a level is one year, 5 levels = 5 years: a child starting Level A at 3 finishes Level E at 8 — but Level E is written for ages 10–12.
- If a level spans its whole age band, Level A must cover ages 3, 4 and 5 with 24 weeks — eight weeks per year.

Our own curriculum map admits the structure is self-imposed, tagged `[original design]`: *"our ladder ends at middle-school readiness **by the 5-level mandate**."* The 5-level mandate was a working constraint, never a finding.

**Your research decides whether we extend the ladder, lengthen each level, or knowingly ship an accelerated path.** Everything above Level A is blocked on the answer, so precision matters more than volume.

## 1. The four questions, in priority order

Answer these. If a question cannot be answered from evidence, say so explicitly and move to Part 4 (construction) for that question only.

**Q1 — How many teaching weeks are in one programme year?** — **CLOSED: 26 per level, level ≈ grade ≈ year.**

live instruction. No calendar anchoring exists in the sources (the prior pass's "Sep–Aug" was a

no evidence either way was found in any source examined.

**Q2 — What is the real level ladder, end to end?** — **CLOSED: 16 booklet-levels ≈ 11 grade-years.**

8 = Algebra. The a/b pairs are halves of one level's 26 weeks, not separate levels (proven at Level 5:
15 + 11 = 26). **Still open:** the modern naming scheme (ME/MF/MG print codes vs in-app "Level M1") —
Speculative, no vector has paid out, and it is cosmetic since we never claim name equivalence.

**Q3 — What is the syllabus at each level?** — **CLOSED for levels 0–5; DEAD for 6–8.**
Recovered by statistical fingerprinting of the 2013 answer keys (they carry answers, no topic labels).
Levels 6/7/8 answer keys were never archived. See the findings §4 and `RESEARCH-DATA-weekly-signal.md`.

**Q4 — How does the ladder sit against school expectations?** — **CLOSED for 0–5: at grade level.**
For each level, the equivalent school grade band, and whether the programme runs **at, ahead of, or behind** grade expectations. Anchor this to a published standards framework (US Common Core State Standards for Mathematics is the default; name whichever you use and cite by standard code, e.g. `1.NBT.B.2`). We need to be able to say "Level C week 14 covers what a school covers in Grade 3 term 2, and adds X."

## 3. How to search — this is hard-won and will save you hours

**Source categories ranked by measured yield from the prior pass:**

1. **`web.archive.org` sweep of the official domain.** Highest yield by a distance. A 2013 answer-key site and archived `/math` tabs recovered an entire historical level ladder that current pages hide. Treat this as a first-class first move, not a fallback. Sweep historical captures of the main site, each programme page, and any `levels`, `curriculum`, `scope`, or `placement` URL you can guess.
2. **App-store listings, screenshots and version history** for any companion app. Screenshots leak grading taxonomy, level→week addressing and test flow.
3. **Official sample worksheets** on marketing CDNs. Worksheet headers often carry level codes.
4. **Teacher/tutor job advertisements and employee reviews.** These leak role split, class composition and level naming that marketing pages omit.
5. **Individual franchise/centre pages.** Far more specific than corporate pages; they publish local schedules, grade-level topic lists and pricing.
6. **Parent-facing portal or handbook PDFs**, enrolment forms, and term calendars — these are where "weeks per year" is most likely to be stated outright.

**Known dead ends — do not budget time here:**
- Review aggregators (Yelp and similar) are usually 403-blocked and were low yield.
- **YouTube transcripts are hard-blocked** from this environment. Seven retrieval routes were tried and all were gated. Use video *metadata, descriptions and thumbnail frames* only.

**Disambiguation, mandatory:** a same-name foreign brand ("Best Brain", Ghana) pollutes results and nearly poisoned the prior ledger. Exclude it explicitly and check the domain on every source before you record it.

**Two techniques that paid:**
- **Contradiction-hunting inside the official corpus.** The marketing copy contradicts itself usefully — "non-repetitive / never review" appears alongside "weekly repetition with increasing complexity". Searching the official corpus for statements that contradict the tagline resolved a structural question with no third-party source at all.
- **Absence is a finding.** If a source category yields nothing, record that as a result and redistribute effort — do not keep grinding it.

## 4. If the evidence does not exist — construct, and label it as construction

If Q2/Q3 cannot be closed from evidence, **build the outline anyway**, because the pipeline downstream needs a scope-and-sequence to author against. Under construction the requirement changes from *"what does this programme do"* to *"what must a programme covering ages 3–14 do, at or above grade expectations, in this pedagogy."*

Construction rules:
- Anchor every constructed week to a **named standard** (e.g. `2.OA.B.2`) plus the term/grade a school teaches it.
- Design to **meet or exceed** grade expectation, and state the margin explicitly per level ("Level C runs one term ahead of Grade 3").
- Preserve the programme's own pedagogy: **one new-or-deepened concept per week**, anti-memorisation, word problems and logic puzzles as the engagement levers, mastery-based advancement.
- **The day arc — read this before assuming anything.** Two separate questions, opposite instructions:
  - **How many days.** The source structure is **7 packets per weekly booklet**, Confirmed
    (`<!-- PACKET-1..7 -->` in the 2013 answer keys, 182 = 26 × 7 exact on every level) and corroborated
    by official copy — weekly class, one booklet section *each day*, submitted end-of-week. The old
    "5 days" rested on 9 marketing images whose day-digit happened to top out at 5 (p ≈ 4.8%). **Do not
    re-research this.** Whether *our* product ships 5 or 7 is a live product decision, not a finding.
  - **What each day does.** The interior arc is `[original design]` (DD3): BB's in-packet day labels
    appear in **no indexed source**. It is unresearchable — there is no success condition. Do not spend
    budget on it. Flag only if a primary packet *interior* ever surfaces.
  - Our current build arc is Day 1 concept · Days 2–3 fluency + discrimination · Day 4 application ·
    Day 5 non-computational + weekly check (`FILL-ARCHITECTURE.md` §3, and the authored `a01.ts`).
    Author against that, not against DD3's older wording.
- Every week must carry a **named misconception** the week teaches against. This is not decoration: the authoring pipeline requires it, and a week without one cannot be built.

## 6. Standards of proof

- **Cite or label.** Every factual claim carries a source or the word Inferred/Speculative. A plausible sentence with no source is the failure mode this brief exists to prevent.
- **Do not smooth over a contradiction.** If two official sources disagree, report both and say which you weight and why. The contradictions have been the most informative findings so far.
- **Distinguish the programme from its marketing.** Marketing claims about pedagogy are evidence about *claims*, not about *practice*.
- **A number without a unit is not an answer.** "24 weeks" must say weeks of what — class meetings, homework sets, or calendar weeks including breaks.
