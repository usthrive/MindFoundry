## 2. The method's parts

Grade mapping used throughout: Singapore Primary 1 (P1) ≈ US Grade 1, P2 ≈ Grade 2, and so on.
Math in Focus (the child's school program) is the US edition of a Singapore series, so the
Singapore sequence is a fair guide to what his lessons contain, but chapter-by-chapter order is
lane R1's job, not this file's.

The ministry's frame for all parts (Confirmed · High, [S1], [S7]): mathematical problem solving
sits at the centre of a pentagon of five components: concepts, skills, processes, metacognition,
attitudes. The ministry's 2023 TIMSS chapter defines metacognition as "awareness, monitoring and
regulation of thought processes" [S7].

### 2.1 Concrete → pictorial → abstract (CPA)

**What it is.** A teaching sequence: the child first acts on real objects (counters, cubes,
place-value chips), then works with pictures that stand for them (drawn disks, ten-frames, bars),
then with symbols alone. The ministry calls CPA "central" to primary and lower-secondary
pedagogy (Confirmed · High, [S7]); the 2007 syllabus says it "is advocated" and that hands-on work
gives "the concrete experience" in the sequence (Confirmed · High, [S1]).

**Origin.** Bruner's three modes of representation (enactive, iconic, symbolic) in *Toward a
Theory of Instruction* (1966). Kho Tek Hong told NIE researchers he adopted CPA at the start of
the ministry's Primary Mathematics Project in 1979–80, and it appeared in the PMP's *Primary
Mathematics* materials first published in 1981 (Confirmed · High, [S2]). Leong, Ho & Cheng also
note that Bruner did not claim one fixed representation per mode, and that the scholarly record on
CPA's actual classroom use is thin (Confirmed · High, [S2]).

**How it is sequenced by grade.** CPA is not a grade sequence; it is repeated inside every new
topic at every primary level (P1–P6), with less time on the concrete stage as the child gets older
and as the topic builds on known ideas (Confirmed for "every level" [S1], [S7]; the
shrinking-concrete pattern is Inferred · Medium from [S2] and practitioner sources). A spiral
curriculum revisits each strand at higher depth each year (Confirmed · High, [S1], [S7]).
A school-level weak point: one practitioner site run for Singapore teachers says time pressure
makes teachers jump to the abstract stage (Speculative · Low,
https://sites.google.com/view/cpamathapproach/home/issues-with-the-c-p-a-approach).

**What the child builds vs only looks at.** In the concrete stage the child *handles* objects;
in the pictorial stage the child *draws* (bars, disks, number bonds), and the textbook pictures
are meant as a bridge, not the end point. The research warning is specific: Leong et al. name two
failure modes, lingering too long in concrete/pictorial work, and skipping it to reach symbols
fast (Confirmed · High, [S2]). For an app, a picture the child only looks at is closer to the
"skip" failure than to CPA.

**Misconceptions targeted.** Treating digits as face values rather than units (the child's Q3/Q5b
rule); not seeing that 10 ones and 1 ten are the same quantity; operations with no meaning
attached. (Inferred · Medium, from [S2], [S4] and the place-value literature in 2.4.)

**Evidence strength.** *Moderate for the general principle, weak for CPA as packaged.*
- Manipulatives vs symbols-only: 55 studies, N = 7,237, small-to-moderate overall advantage;
  retention moderate-to-large, but transfer and problem solving only small; effects depend on
  how the teaching is done (Confirmed (abstract) · High, [S4]).
- Concreteness fading (concrete → idealised → abstract, faded explicitly) is argued to beat
  either end alone, especially for transfer (Inferred · Medium, [S5]); a 2021 analysis says the
  claim may generalise less than suggested (Inferred · Medium, [S5]).
- The US "CRA" (concrete-representational-abstract) literature shows positive effects for
  students with learning difficulties in computation, fractions, word problems (as cited by
  [S2]; Inferred · Medium).
- No study isolates CPA as a Singapore package; its effect is folded into whole-curriculum
  studies (section 3). (Absence; Confirmed by search, Medium.)

### 2.2 Number bonds (part-whole)

**What it is.** A small diagram of a whole joined to its two (later more) parts, e.g. 10 split
into 7 and 3. One bond carries a whole fact family: two addition and two subtraction sentences.
It is the first pictorial form of the part-whole idea that the bar model later scales up.

**Origin.** The 2007 syllabus places "building up the addition bonds up to 9 + 9 and committing
to memory" in P1 (Confirmed · High, [S1]). Number bonds as a named diagram are a Singapore
textbook feature; the underlying idea is the "part-part-whole" construct of US/UK research of the
1980s (Resnick; Fischer 1990, *JRME* 21(3) 207–215, a kindergarten part-part-whole curriculum)
(Inferred · Medium, [S9]). We found no source naming who first drew the bond diagram
(absence).

**How it is sequenced by grade.**
- P1 / Grade 1: bonds of numbers to 10, then to 20; bonds are memorised (Confirmed for
  "to 9 + 9, committing to memory" [S1]; "bonds to 10 in P1" from a parent-facing summary of
  the 2021 syllabus, Inferred · Medium, [S10]).
- P1–P2: bonds drive the mental strategies (make ten: 8 + 5 → 8 + 2 + 3) and the move from
  "take away" to "find the missing part" (Inferred · Medium, practitioner sources [S10]).
- P2 onward: the bond's job passes to the bar model for word problems, and to place-value
  decomposition (652 = 600 + 50 + 2) for multi-digit work (Inferred · Medium).

**What the child builds vs only looks at.** In Singapore practice the child *splits a real
set* first, then *fills* the empty circle of a drawn bond, then writes the matching sentences
(Inferred · Medium, [S10]). The fill-the-missing-part act is the point: it is the same
structure as 635 + ? = 652.

**Misconceptions targeted.** "Subtraction is only take-away"; addition and subtraction as
unrelated; a missing-addend item read as "do something to the two numbers shown" (the child's
Q3 answer 23). (Inferred · Medium.)

**Evidence strength.** *Weak to moderate, and indirect.* Part-whole understanding is
well-established as a foundation of early arithmetic word-problem solving in cognitive research
(Inferred · Medium, citing papers to Fischer 1990 and Resnick [S9]); we found **no controlled
study of number bonds as a diagram** (absence; Confirmed by search, Medium). Search results for
"number bond evidence" returned only teaching guides and vendor pages (Confirmed, Medium).

### 2.3 The model (bar) method

**What it is.** The child draws rectangles (bars) to stand for quantities, sized roughly to
their relative values, labels the known and unknown parts, and reads the operation off the
picture. Three basic forms: **part-whole** (one bar split into parts), **comparison** (two
or more bars side by side, the difference marked), and **change** / before-after; then the same
forms for multiplication and division (equal units), fractions (a bar cut into equal units),
ratio and percentage (Inferred · Medium-High: Kho 1987 as cited by NIE researchers [S11];
Hoven & Garelick [S12]; a Singapore school's 2026 parent deck [S13]).

**Origin and history.**
- Devised in the ministry's Curriculum Planning / Primary Mathematics Project work under
  Kho Tek Hong; first presented in Kho (1987), "Mathematical models for solving arithmetic
  problems", ICMI-SEAMS 4th conference proceedings, Singapore Institute of Education,
  pp. 345–351 (Inferred · High: cited the same way by several NIE papers [S11], [S6]).
- Start date is disputed in the sources: "introduced in 1983" and "late 1980s for P5–P6, now
  from P1" are both given by Berinderjeet Kaur in different documents (Inferred · Medium,
  [S11]). Our reading: the method entered textbooks in the 1980s and spread down the grades.
- **Why it was invented.** Kaur (2019, *ZDM*) dates the method's entry into the primary
  curriculum to 1983 and ties it to a 1975 ministry study: only 46% of pupils could solve word
  problems that lacked cue words such as "altogether" or "left" (Inferred (abstract via search)
  · Medium-High, [S8]). The bar model was built to replace keyword reading with structure.
- The ministry's own monograph, *The Singapore Model Method for Learning Mathematics*
  (Kho, Yeo & Lim; CPDD, MOE 2009; published by Marshall Cavendish's Panpac imprint), was
  launched in the US on 22 April 2009 explicitly for teachers using *Primary Mathematics* or
  "considering Math in Focus" (Confirmed · High, ministry/IE Singapore media release [S14]).

**How it is sequenced by grade** (Singapore P-levels; US grade in brackets).
- P1–P2 [G1–G2]: part-whole and comparison bars for one-step addition and subtraction; a
  Singapore school's 2026 parent workshop shows a P2 "within 1,000" problem solved with a
  part-whole model (Confirmed for that school · Medium, [S13]).
- P3 [G3]: two-step problems with a bracketed model; multiplication and division as equal
  units (Confirmed via Hoven & Garelick's reading of the textbooks · Medium, [S12]).
- P4 [G4]: fractions; a fractional part becomes the unit (Confirmed via [S12] · Medium).
- P5–P6 [G5–G6]: ratio, percentage, before-after and "algebraic" word problems that the bar
  model lets children solve before letter algebra (Confirmed · High, [S6]).
- Beckmann's reading of the Singapore *Primary Mathematics* texts: strip diagrams accompany
  story problems from the first half of Grade 3 (volume 3A) across all four operations,
  fractions and decimals; **algebra story problems begin in Grade 4**, e.g. two groups from
  their total and difference, solved by 4th graders with a strip diagram (Confirmed · High,
  [S46]). The child's Q7 (two numbers from sum 810 and difference 100) is this Grade-4 type
  on a Grade-2 test (Inferred · Medium).
- Secondary: a planned hand-over from bars to letter-symbolic algebra (Inferred · Medium,
  NIE SingTeach [S15]).

**What the child builds vs only looks at.** The scaffold fades in a fixed order: early items
*supply* the bar model; later items *require the child to build it* (Confirmed via
Hoven & Garelick's description of the textbooks · Medium, [S12]). This is the key design
fact: a module that only shows finished bars stops at the first rung.

**Misconceptions targeted.** Picking an operation from surface words; "less than" read as
"subtract" when the unknown is the larger quantity; losing one of two conditions in
two-condition problems (sum-and-difference, the child's Q7). (Inferred · Medium.)

**Evidence strength.** *Moderate for diagram-based schema instruction in general (section 3);
descriptive only for the Singapore bar model itself.*
- Ng & Lee (2009): descriptive study, 151 P5 children; the method gives children without
  letter algebra a way to represent algebraic problems (Confirmed (abstract) · Medium-High,
  [S6]). An earlier NIE study of 151 P5 pupils found success fell as problems' algebraic
  structure got less uniform (Inferred · Medium, [S11]).
- Lee, Ng & Ng (2009), *J. Educ. Psych.*: working memory explained about a quarter of the
  variance in both drawing the model and solving (Inferred · Medium, as reported by search
  summaries of the NIE record [S16]). Design implication: the drawing itself costs working
  memory, so a tool that does the drawing mechanics lowers the load.
- Morin (2014 dissertation; 2017 *Learning Disability Quarterly* 40(2) 91–104): bar-model
  drawing taught explicitly (paraphrase, visualise, hypothesise, check) to students with
  maths difficulties; multiple-baseline across groups; strategy use and accuracy rose and held
  (Confirmed (abstract) · Medium, [S17]). Small-n single-case design.
- **Critiques.** (a) Hoven & Garelick: the bar model "would not necessarily be meaningful"
  without the underlying concepts (Confirmed · Medium, [S12]). (b) Secondary teachers report
  students clinging to models when algebra is due; NIE advises asking what each rectangle
  stands for (Inferred · Medium, [S15]). (c) Arithmetic habits interfere with algebra; strategy
  choice depends on inhibition and working memory (Inferred · Medium, NIE 157-pupil study,
  [S16]). (d) The 2026 Singapore school deck teaches "keywords" (gave, left) to pick the
  model, a practice US researchers warn against (see 2.7; Confirmed that the deck does it ·
  Medium, [S13]).

### 2.4 Place-value disks / chips and how regrouping is taught

**What it is.** Base-ten blocks are *proportional* (a ten-rod is ten unit cubes long); place-value
disks (US: "chips") are *non-proportional*: identical disks marked 1, 10, 100, whose value comes
only from the label or column. Regrouping (Singapore textbook word: **"renaming"**, used in the
2007 syllabus, Confirmed · High [S1]) is acted out as a trade: ten 1-disks become one 10-disk
(addition), or one 10-disk is broken into ten 1-disks (subtraction), on a place-value chart,
before and alongside the written column.

**How it is sequenced by grade.**
- P1 [G1]: tens and ones with bundles/cubes; addition and subtraction of two 2-digit numbers
  **with formal algorithms already in P1** (Confirmed · High, [S1]).
- P2 [G2]: numbers to 1,000; written addition and subtraction to 3 digits; hundreds-tens-ones
  materials (Confirmed for content · High, [S1]; which material when, Speculative · Low).
- P3–P4: disks carry the place-value idea to 4–5 digits and decimals, where proportional blocks
  become impractical (Inferred · Medium, retailer/practitioner sources, e.g.
  https://www.singaporemath.com/products/10-value-decimal-place-value-discs).
- Which grade Math in Focus moves from blocks to chips was **not found publicly** (lane R1).

**What the child builds vs only looks at.** The child physically makes the trade and *records
it at the moment it happens* (the small carried 1 = "one new ten"); Eureka Math, a US program
of similar lineage, has the child *draw* chips and "bundle" them (Inferred · Medium, [S18]).
A row of printed disks the child only looks at does not make the trade.

**Misconceptions targeted (with research).**
- **Digits as face values, each column alone.** Brown & Burton's BUGGY work catalogued
  systematic multi-column "bugs"; the best known, *smaller-from-larger*, subtracts the smaller
  digit from the larger in each column regardless of which is on top (Confirmed via the
  paper's abstract and citing papers · High, [S19]). This is exactly the child's Q3 rule
  (652 vs 635 → 023). Secondary summaries say about 40% of students in their data showed
  consistent bugs, mostly in borrowing (Inferred · Low-Medium, [S19]; not checked against
  the original).
- **Bugs are repairs.** Brown & VanLehn's *Repair Theory* (1980) and VanLehn's *Mind Bugs*
  (MIT Press, 1990): when a learned procedure reaches an impasse, the child patches it; different
  patches give different bugs, and bugs "migrate" over days (Confirmed via the papers' own
  descriptions · Medium-High, [S20]). Design implication: one wrong answer is a patch, not a
  stable belief; a second probe item of the same structure is needed before labelling.
- **Which conception of a two-digit number the child holds.** Fuson et al. (1997, *JRME*
  28(2) 130–162), drawing on four US projects, name five conceptual structures (unitary,
  decade-and-ones, sequence tens-and-ones, separate tens-and-ones, integrated); they recommend
  sustained work linking ten-based **quantities** to number **words** and **numerals**
  ("triads") and tie typical errors to each method (Confirmed (abstract) · Medium-High, [S21]).
- **Invention before algorithm.** Carpenter, Franke, Jacobs, Fennema & Empson (1998, *JRME*
  29(1) 3–20): 82 children followed Grades 1–3, interviewed 5 times; about 90% used invented
  strategies; those who invented strategies *before* learning standard algorithms showed better
  base-ten knowledge and transfer (Confirmed (abstract) · High for the finding, Medium for
  causation: longitudinal, not randomised, [S22]).
- **Regrouping as "decomposing a higher-value unit".** Liping Ma (1999): of 23 US teachers most
  taught a rote "borrowing" rule, while many of 72 Chinese teachers framed the step as
  decomposing a higher-value unit and grounded it in subtraction within 10 and 20 (Confirmed via
  publisher ToC and an independent review · High, [S23], [S24]). Carry and borrow are then
  *one* idea (compose / decompose a unit), which is exactly what the child's Q2 and Q3 need.

**Evidence strength.** *Strong* that multi-digit errors are systematic and rooted in weak
place-value meaning (bug research, Fuson, Carpenter; multiple research programs agree).
*Moderate* for manipulatives in general [S4]. **No study found comparing place-value disks with
base-ten blocks** (absence; Medium).

### 2.5 Mental strategies, and where Singapore puts mental vs written

**What it is.** Calculating in the head with named strategies: **make ten** (8 + 5 = 8 + 2 + 3),
**compensation** (49 + 26 = 50 + 26 − 1), **left-to-right / "add the tens then the ones"**
(34 + 25 = 50 + 9), adding on 10s or 100s, doubles and near doubles.

**Where Singapore puts it** (Confirmed · High for the **2007** syllabus rows [S1]; the 2021
syllabus text could not be reached, so the current placement is unconfirmed):

| Level | Mental calculation row in the 2007 syllabus | Written in the same year |
|---|---|---|
| P1 [G1] | add/subtract within 20; 2-digit and ones **without renaming**; 2-digit and tens | two 2-digit numbers by formal algorithm |
| P2 [G2] | 3-digit and ones, tens or hundreds; × ÷ within tables of 2, 3, 4, 5, 10 | numbers up to 3 digits; up to 2-step word problems |
| P3 [G3] | **two 2-digit numbers** | numbers up to 4 digits |

So Singapore teaches the written column early (P1) and keeps mental work to cases that need no
renaming, or to adding a single unit (ones / tens / hundreds), until P3. It is **not**
"mental first" like the Dutch approach below. This makes the child's Q1 ("add mentally", two
2-digit numbers with regrouping, in Grade 2) a US-standards demand (CCSS 2.NBT.5) one year ahead
of where the Singapore ministry syllabus places that mental skill (Inferred · Medium: the
Grade-2 placement in the US edition is from his paper; the Singapore placement is Confirmed).

**Research on the strategies.**
- **Make ten.** Fuson & Kwon (1992, *JRME* 23, 148–165): Korean children solved almost two-thirds
  of sums above 10 by ten-based recomposition or known facts, helped by regular ten-based number
  words; a later review cautions language may not be the main cause (Confirmed (abstract) ·
  Medium-High, [S25]).
- **Two ways to add two-digit numbers in the head.** Beishuizen (1993, *JRME* 24(4) 294–323), Dutch
  Grade 2: *N10* (keep one number whole, jump on by tens: 34 + 25 → 54 → 59) is more effective and
  less error-prone but harder to learn; *1010* (split both into tens and ones, the
  "tens-then-ones" strategy) is preferred by weaker children; recommend teaching both. Klein,
  Beishuizen & Treffers (1998, *JRME* 29(4), N = 275) compared two Grade-2 programs built on the
  empty number line (Confirmed (abstract) · Medium-High, [S26]). Design implication: the 1010
  strategy is the mental cousin of the column (and of his Kumon habit); N10 on an open number
  line is the strategy that breaks the column reflex.
- **Invented strategies can harden into new rote algorithms if handed out as recipes** (NCTM
  blog citing Carpenter et al.; Inferred · Medium, https://nctm.org/Publications/TCM-blog/Blog/Strategies-Are-Not-Algorithms).

**What the child builds vs only looks at.** The child *chooses* a strategy and *shows the
jumps* (number line hops, a split bond) before giving the answer; a typed answer alone cannot
show whether a column was done in the head (the child's Q1 shows exactly this).

**Misconceptions targeted.** "Every sum is a column"; counting on by ones for large numbers;
not knowing that 49 is "one less than 50".

**Evidence strength.** *Moderate*: descriptive and quasi-experimental studies across the
Netherlands, Korea and the US agree that ten-based strategies are efficient and teachable; flexible
choice among strategies is harder to produce than any one strategy (Inferred · Medium).

### 2.6 Systematic variation (Dienes)

**What it is.** Two principles from Zoltan Dienes (1960s): **perceptual variability**: meet the
same structure in many surface forms (disks, bars, money, a number line) so the child abstracts
what they share; **mathematical variability**: vary every variable that is not essential to
the concept (which quantity is unknown, the size of numbers, whether regrouping occurs) so the
child generalises it (Inferred · Medium-High, Lindorff, Hall & Sammons 2019 [S27]; Singapore
lineage publisher pages). In Singapore-style books it shows up as short practice sets where
one feature changes at a time (e.g. the same three numbers as part-whole, missing part,
missing whole), not as long sets of identical items.

**Origin and sequencing.** Dienes' principles are cited as the basis for variation in
Singapore-lineage materials, together with Bruner's CPA (Inferred · Medium, [S27]). There is no
grade sequence; it is a design rule for every exercise set. Note the related but separate East
Asian "variation theory" (Marton; Chinese *bianshi*), which UK "mastery" materials also invoke
(Speculative · Low; not researched further in this lane).

**What the child builds vs only looks at.** The child solves a *set* designed so that the
answer to item n helps with item n+1, and is asked what changed; the variation is wasted if the
items are shown one at a time with no comparison (Inferred · Medium).

**Misconceptions targeted.** Over-generalising a just-taught procedure. The 2021 IES guide
makes the same point with a regrouping example: once regrouping is taught, mix in items that do
**not** need it, so students must discriminate (Confirmed · High, [S28]). Mixed structure
(unknown in different positions, 2 vs 4 addends) is exactly what the child's Q2, Q3, Q5b varied.

**Evidence strength.** *Weak for Dienes-style variation as a named package* (no isolating study
found; absence). *Strong* for the narrower practice of mixing problem types so students must
discriminate (IES 2021, Recommendation 1, strong evidence, 43 studies [S28]).

### 2.7 Heuristics (the ministry list; Pólya)

**What it is.** General problem-solving moves taught by name. The ministry's 2007 list groups
them in four kinds (Confirmed · High, [S1]):
1. *give a representation*: draw a diagram, make a list, use equations;
2. *make a calculated guess*: guess and check, look for patterns, make suppositions;
3. *go through the process*: act it out, work backwards, before-after;
4. *change the problem*: restate, simplify, solve part of the problem.
"Draw a diagram" is where the model method sits. A four-phase process (understand, plan,
carry out, look back) from Pólya's *How to Solve It* (Princeton UP, first published 1945) is
the usual frame (Confirmed for the book and its heuristic dictionary · High, [S29]; the
four phases are well documented but not on the publisher's page, Inferred · High). Schools
adapt it: the 2026 Singapore school deck teaches "R.I.G.H.T." (Read, Identify keywords, Get a
plan, Have it done, Triple check) (Confirmed for that school · Medium, [S13]).

**Origin and sequencing.** The ministry's problem-solving framework was published in 1990 and the
problem-solving curriculum ran from 1992; a 1997 "Thinking Schools, Learning Nation" push and
a 2001 revision added explicit heuristics and thinking skills; later reforms shift from
teaching heuristics to "habits of mind" incl. metacognition (Confirmed via Yeap Ban Har's
ICME-11 paper, reproduced with permission [S30]; Inferred · Medium-High via Dindyal, MERGA
2006 [S31]). Grade sequence: no primary source found. Our working guess (Speculative · Low): P1–P2 act it
out, draw a picture/model, look for a pattern; P3–P4 guess and check, make a list,
before-after; P5–P6 work backwards, supposition, simplify.

**What the child builds vs only looks at.** The child names the move and carries it out on
his own paper; a heuristic shown as a finished worked example and never chosen by the child
is a recipe, not a heuristic.

**Misconceptions targeted.** Freezing when no rule fits; one-track attempts. Yeap documents
P6 pupils who planned the model method, drew a wrong model and did not switch plans for 15
minutes (Confirmed · Medium, [S30]): heuristics without monitoring fail.

**Evidence strength.** *Mixed.*
- The Singapore textbooks lean on pictorial heuristics; diagram heuristics helped many pupils
  on hard national-exam items (descriptive; Confirmed · Medium, [S30]).
- The IES 2012 guide for Grades 4–8 includes "teach students how to use visual representations"
  and "help students monitor and reflect" among five recommendations (Confirmed for the list ·
  High; evidence level per recommendation not read, [S32]).
- **The keyword strategy that many schools teach as a heuristic is explicitly advised against**
  by the IES 2021 panel: key words skip reading the problem and often point to the wrong
  operation; multi-step problems contain several (Confirmed · High, [S28]; citing Karp, Bush &
  Dougherty 2019, "Avoiding the ineffective keyword strategy"). The same guide adds that one
  problem type can need different operations, so a type should not be tied to an operation
  (Confirmed · High, [S28]). The Singapore school deck's "Identify keywords" step conflicts
  with this.
- Evidence that *general* heuristics (Pólya) transfer to young children is thin: small
  quasi-experiments and an unvetted review (Speculative · Low; absence of strong studies).
  Domain-specific schemas (2.3, section 3) have much stronger evidence than general heuristics.

### 2.8 Metacognition and "explain your thinking" / journal routines

**What it is.** The ministry defines metacognition as awareness, monitoring and regulation of
one's thinking (Confirmed · High, [S7]); it has been a named component of the framework since
1990 (Inferred · Medium-High, [S31]). Classroom forms: think-aloud questioning ("How do you know?
Is there another method?" in the 2026 school deck, Confirmed · Medium, [S13]); checking the answer
against the problem; math journals (short written reflections or explanations); "log writing"
promoted by NIE's Lee Ngan Hoe (Inferred · Medium, [S33]). NIE has published a book, *Journal
Writing in the Mathematics Classroom (Primary)* (Inferred · Low-Medium: known only from a blog,
[S33]).

**Sequencing.** No grade sequence was found; journals and think-alouds appear from P1 in
practitioner accounts, written explanations growing longer with age (Speculative · Low).

**What the child builds vs only looks at.** The child *produces* the explanation (spoken or
written), ideally answering one focused prompt ("Why is 240 written with a zero under the
tens?"), rather than choosing from offered explanations.

**Misconceptions targeted.** Not checking an answer against the conditions (the child's Q7:
700 + 100 = 800, never checked against 810); describing *where* digits go without *why* (Q6).

**Evidence strength.** *Moderate for prompted self-explanation; weak for journals as such.*
- Rittle-Johnson, Loehr & Durkin (2017, *ZDM* 49, 599–611), meta-analysis: prompted
  self-explanation gave small-to-moderate gains in procedural knowledge, conceptual knowledge and
  procedural transfer, but in "numerous cases" did not help or even hurt; advice: scaffold
  explanations (training or structured response formats) and do not let prompts crowd out
  practice (Confirmed (abstract) · High, [S34]).
- No Singapore trial isolating journal writing was found (absence; Medium). An NIE study of P5
  and Sec 1 pupils' metacognitive awareness called for structured programmes to be developed
  and researched (Inferred · Medium, [S33]).

### 3.6 Critiques and limits (collected)

1. **Effects of whole Singapore-lineage curricula are small** (+0.10 to +0.15 SD in RCTs; one
   small UK RCT ES 0.42 late in the year) [S37], [S38], [S40], [S27]. Large effects come from
   targeted schema tutoring with researcher-made tests [S42], [S44]; against strong active
   comparisons the edge shrinks to near zero [S45]. (Confirmed · High.)
2. **Implementation fidelity and teacher knowledge.** US pilots failed or were mixed where
   training was thin, materials late, turnover high, or the book was used without the system
   [S3], [S41]. Teachers first used the bar model as decoration on a solved problem [S3].
   Ma's teachers show why: regrouping taught as a rule, not as unit decomposition [S23].
   Singapore textbooks do not reteach prior content, so gaps compound [S3]. (Confirmed · High.)
3. **Bar-model over-use and transfer.** Bars without the concepts are not meaningful [S12];
   pupils stick to a wrong model instead of switching plans [S30]; at secondary they resist
   letter algebra [S15], [S16]; working-memory load is high [S16]. (Confirmed/Inferred ·
   Medium.)
4. **Keyword cues inside "heuristic" routines** contradict the strongest US evidence [S28],
   [S13]. (Confirmed · High.)
5. **Math in Focus Grade 2 alignment.** Chapter tests contain above-grade items; some Grade-2
   standards get little practice, including adding up to four 2-digit numbers [S39].
   (Confirmed · High.)
6. **CPA as packaged lacks direct study**; the scholarly record on its classroom use is thin
   [S2]; concreteness fading may generalise less than claimed [S5]. (Confirmed/Inferred ·
   Medium.)
7. **Attitude dip**: Year-1 pupils new to Inspire Maths were more negative in term 1 [S27].
   (Confirmed · Medium; one study.)

## 6. Summary

### What we found

1. **CPA is the ministry's central primary pedagogy**, adopted by Kho Tek Hong at the start of
   the Primary Mathematics Project (1979–80), from Bruner; it repeats inside every topic, not
   across grades [S1], [S2], [S7]. Its general principle has moderate support (manipulatives
   meta-analysis; concreteness fading); CPA as a package has not been studied directly [S4], [S5].
2. **The bar model was built to replace keyword reading** (1975 study: 46% could solve problems
   without cue words), entered the curriculum in the 1980s, first written up by Kho (1987); three
   basic forms (part-whole, comparison, change), then equal units, fractions, ratio [S8], [S11].
   Scaffolds fade from supplied models to child-built models [S12]; algebra-type problems start
   in Grade 4 [S46].
3. **Singapore teaches the written column early (P1) and restricts mental calculation** to
   no-renaming cases and adding single units until P3 (two 2-digit numbers mentally) [S1]
   (2007 syllabus; the 2021 text was not reachable). It is
   not a "mental first" approach.
4. **Strongest US evidence that matches Singapore features**: IES 2021 practice guide, all six
   recommendations strong: systematic instruction, mathematical language, concrete and
   semi-concrete representations, number lines, word problems taught by schema type with the
   unknown in every position, timed fluency activities; and *avoid key words* [S28].
5. **Schema instruction effects are large on researcher measures** (g 1.57 / 1.09 transfer;
   Fuchs ES 1.80) and positive for Grades 1–2 [S42], [S43], [S44], but nearly vanish against a
   strong active comparison [S45].
6. **Whole Singapore-lineage curricula show small effects**: Math in Focus cluster RCT +0.11 to
   +0.15 SD, equal on procedures and problem solving [S37]; Evidence for ESSA "Strong", +0.15
   [S38]; Inspire Maths (England) ES 0.42 in term 3 only, attitudes dipped in term 1 [S27]; EEF
   Mathematics Mastery +0.10 n.s. [S40]. **No WWC review of Math in Focus exists that we could find.**
7. **Implementation is the recurring failure**: US pilots mixed; teachers used the bar model as a
   picture on a solved problem in year 1; thin training, late materials, turnover [S3], [S41].
8. **Multi-digit errors are systematic bugs** (smaller-from-larger = the child's Q3 rule), arise
   as repairs, and migrate; place-value conceptions and invention-before-algorithm research explain
   why [S19]–[S22]. Ma frames regrouping as composing/decomposing a higher-value unit [S23], [S24].
9. **Math in Focus Grade 2 has known alignment gaps**: above-grade items on chapter tests and thin
   practice on 2.NBT.6 (the child's Q2 skill) [S39].
10. **Books**: the ministry monograph (B1) gives the model taxonomy but no grade sequence or lesson
    plans; Beckmann (B5, plus her open 2004 article) is the best public source for which diagram at
    which grade; Ma (B4) for the regrouping explanation; Fong Ho Kheong (B7) is the principal author
    of the series behind Math in Focus.

### What we could not find publicly

1. **The current (2021) ministry syllabus text** for P1–P6: school-hosted copies returned 404; the
   2007 syllabus and the ministry's 2023 TIMSS chapter were used instead. Exact current wording on
   heuristics, Pólya and mental calculation is unconfirmed.
2. **A grade-by-grade sequence of model types from a primary source** (when comparison models,
   equal-unit models, before-after begin). Only practitioner and Beckmann's textbook reading.
3. **Any controlled study of number bonds as a diagram**, of place-value disks vs base-ten blocks,
   or of Dienes-style variation as a package.
4. **A What Works Clearinghouse review of Math in Focus** or of any Singapore-lineage curriculum.
5. **Full texts** of Ng & Lee (2009), Kaur (2019), Fyfe et al. (2014), Peltier & Vannest (2017)
   moderators, Carpenter et al. (1998), Fuson et al. (1997), Brown & Burton (1978): only abstracts
   or citing summaries were legally reachable; Brown & Burton bug prevalence (~40%) is second-hand.
6. **The 2009 ministry monograph's contents beyond a retailer summary**; Yeap's *Bar Modeling*
   table of contents; any open chapter of *The Singapore Journey*; any confirmed model-method book
   by Fong Ho Kheong.
7. **Which grade Math in Focus moves from base-ten blocks to place-value chips**, and whether its
   Grade 2 lessons ask the child to *draw* bar models or only read them (lane R1 / lesson pages).
8. **Any trial isolating math-journal writing** in Singapore or the US.
9. **Primary evidence that general heuristics (Pólya) transfer for Grades 1–3**: only small or
   unvetted studies.
10. **The Montgomery County district evaluation itself** (PDF is encrypted); its findings are known
    only through Garelick (2006) and the AIR report.
